Notes  ·  Dimensional inspection

Fitting a measured part to its CAD model

How the alignment problem is set up, why the obvious formulation cannot be solved, what replacing points by measures buys, and how the resulting landscape is traversed.

01 — The situationTwo things are unknown, and they depend on each other

A part comes off the machine and we measure it. A touch probe or a scanner returns points x₁, …, x_N ∈ ℝ³, expressed in the frame of the measuring machine. Separately we have the part's CAD model: an exact surface Y ⊂ ℝ³, expressed in the design frame. Nobody recorded how the part was sitting on the table when it was measured.

Before we can say anything about whether the part is within tolerance, the two descriptions have to be brought into one frame. That means finding a rigid motion T ∈ SE(3) — a rotation together with a translation, six degrees of freedom, no scaling and no bending — that carries the measured points into the design frame so that they land on the modeled surface.

So there are two unknowns:

  1. the pose T;
  2. for each measured point, the location on the surface it came from.

Either one is easy given the other. With the correspondences known, the pose follows from a small closed-form fit. With the pose known, each correspondence is a nearest-point query against the surface. We start with neither, and that is the whole difficulty.

These notes proceed in four moves: describe what Y actually is; write the joint problem down faithfully and identify precisely what makes it intractable; replace each unknown surface location by a probability measure, which removes those obstructions without changing the answer; and then eliminate the measures again to get a descent method on the pose alone. The algorithm that falls out is ICP. The reason for taking the long route is that the route, not the algorithm, explains what the algorithm can and cannot do.

02 — The modelWhat Y is, in enough detail to matter

A STEP model stores the part as a boundary representation, or B-rep, which has two layers.

Geometry is the surfaces themselves. Each one is a map S : Ω → ℝ³ from a rectangle of parameters Ω ⊂ ℝ² into space, written S(u,v). The catalogue is small and analytic for the most part: planes, cylinders, cones, spheres, tori, plus NURBS patches for freeform shape. Curves work the same way, as maps C(t) from an interval into space.

Topology is what has been trimmed away and what is glued to what. A face is a surface together with closed loops drawn in its (u,v) domain, marking off the portion that is kept. An edge is a curve shared by two faces. A vertex is a point shared by edges. Faces sewn together along their edges bound a closed solid.

Two consequences of this description drive everything that follows.

The first is that Y is a compact two-dimensional surface sitting in ℝ³: exact and described rather than sampled. There is no mesh and no point set. Asking for the closest point of Y to a given location in space is a well-posed geometric query, answered face by face, and its answer is exact up to floating point. That query will be the workhorse later.

The second is that Y is an awkward place to do calculus. It carries no linear structure, because the average of two locations on a curved surface is generally not on the surface. And it admits coordinates only one face at a time: the (u,v) parameterization is cut off at trimming boundaries and degenerates outright at cone apices and sphere poles.

Carry forwardY is exact and easy to query, but it has no linear structure and no single coordinate system.

03 — The joint problemThe faithful statement, and why it cannot be solved

The physical story is simple. Each measurement came from one particular location yᵢ ∈ Y on the modeled surface, and was observed with noise after the part was placed by some unknown T. Writing the total squared deviation:

E(T, y₁, …, y_N)  =  Σᵢ ‖T(xᵢ) − yᵢ‖² minimize over T ∈ SE(3) and y₁,…,y_N ∈ Y
(1)

This is the correct problem. It is also unusable, and it pays to be exact about why, because the repair is aimed at these specific points and nothing else.

The pose variable is not the problem. SE(3) is a smooth six-dimensional group, and the objective is an ordinary quadratic in it. Every obstruction lives in the correspondence variables y₁,…,y_N, and there are three of them.

There is nothing to move along. Since Y is curved, a step taken from a surface location leaves the surface, and the average of two surface locations is not a surface location. There is no convex combination to form and no descent direction that stays feasible without a projection back onto the surface.

Coordinates break exactly where measurements land. Doing calculus in yᵢ requires a chart, charts exist only per face, and those charts stop at trimming boundaries and degenerate at apices and poles. Measurements near edges and fillets — which is to say a large fraction of them — sit close to these failures.

The choice of face is discrete. This is the real obstruction. Which face yᵢ lies on is a combinatorial variable, and sliding a correspondence from one face to a neighbouring one is not continuous motion within any single chart; it is a jump from one chart to another. With K faces and N measurements there are Kᴺ branches, each containing its own continuous refinement problem.

Carry forwardThe objective in (1) is friendly — a sum of squares. The feasible set Yᴺ is what makes the problem hard, so the feasible set is what we change.

04 — The relaxationReplacing a location by a distribution of weight

Hold the pose T fixed and look at a single measured point x. Write f(y) = ‖T(x) − y‖², a continuous function on the compact surface Y: the cost of blaming the location y for this measurement. Originally we choose one location and pay the cost there.

Instead, allow a choice of probability measure π on Y — an assignment of weight totalling one, spread over the surface however we like — and pay the weighted average ∫ f dπ. Write P(Y) for the set of all such measures. The claim is that this changes nothing:

miny ∈ Y f(y)  =  minπ ∈ P(Y) ∫Y f dπ
(2)

Both directions are immediate. No weighted average of values can fall below the smallest value, and the weights total one, so ∫ f dπ ≥ min f for every π — the right side cannot be smaller than the left. In the other direction, the measure that puts all its weight at a minimizing location y*, written δ_{y*}, pays exactly f(y*) = min f — so the right side cannot be larger either. The two are equal.

Every relaxation is a bet that enlarging the search space does not lower the optimum. Here the bet is settled in advance: identity (2) is exact, not an approximation or a bound.

Aside — why the weights have to be measures

Cost is a value at a location, so it is a function on Y. Weight is an amount over a region, and it cannot be a function on Y, because on a continuum a single point occupies no area and would carry none.

So ask instead what the weight object has to do. It takes a cost landscape and returns a single bill. It does so linearly — double every cost and the bill doubles — and continuously — a small change in costs makes a small change in the bill. That is precisely a bounded linear functional on C(Y), the continuous functions on Y. The Riesz–Markov theorem says that on a compact space every such functional is integration against a unique regular Borel measure, and that the correspondence M(Y) ≅ C(Y)* preserves norms exactly. Being an object of that kind and being a measure are the same thing, so nothing was assumed by writing ∫ f dπ.

05 — The relaxed problemWhat the enlargement buys

Applying (2) to every measurement at once gives the relaxed energy. Each correspondence yᵢ ∈ Y has become a measure πᵢ ∈ P(Y):

E(T, π₁, …, π_N)  =  Σᵢ ∫Y ‖T(xᵢ) − y‖² dπᵢ(y) minimize over T ∈ SE(3) and π₁,…,π_N ∈ P(Y)
(3)

The search space is far larger — each πᵢ is infinite-dimensional where yᵢ was three numbers — and yet every obstruction from §03 is gone.

There is now something to move along. P(Y) is convex: blend two probability measures and the result is a probability measure. Straight lines between feasible points stay feasible, which is exactly what failed for locations on a curved surface.

The objective is linear in each correspondence variable. An integral is linear in the measure it integrates against, no matter how badly behaved the cost function is. This holds exactly, not after a change of variables or a local approximation.

The discrete face choice dissolves. A measure with half its weight on face A and half on face B is an unremarkable element of P(Y), whereas no location was half on each. Moving a correspondence from one face to another is now a continuous slide of weight through legitimate intermediate states. The Kᴺ branches have become one connected convex set.

The coordinate degeneracies stop mattering. A measure does not need a chart. The point mass δ_q is the same kind of object whether q lies in the interior of a face, on an edge curve, at a vertex, or at a cone apex, so trimming boundaries and poles no longer require special handling.

A minimizer exists. P(Y) sits inside the unit ball of M(Y) = C(Y)*, which is compact in the weak-* topology by Banach–Alaoglu, and P(Y) is a closed subset of it, hence itself compact. The pose is not confined to a compact set, but the energy grows without bound as the translation runs off to infinity, so the search can be restricted to a bounded sublevel set at no cost. A continuous function on a compact set attains its minimum, so problem (3) has a solution.

Carry forwardProblem (3) has the same optimal value as (1), a convex feasible set in the correspondence variables, an objective linear in them, no face branching, and a guaranteed minimizer.

06 — IndependenceThe N correspondence problems do not interact

Nothing in problem (3) restricts what the model side receives. There is no bound on Σᵢ πᵢ(A) for a region A of the surface, so two measurements are free to place all their weight on the same location, and a hundred are free to pile onto one face while another face receives nothing.

That absence has a structural consequence. The feasible set for the correspondences is a plain product ∏ᵢ P(Y), in which each coordinate can be chosen without regard to the others. A sum of terms minimized over a product set separates:

min(π₁,…,π_N) Σᵢ (cost of measurement i)  =  Σᵢ minπᵢ (cost of measurement i)
(4)

So for a fixed pose, the correspondence problem is not one problem in N coupled variables but N separate one-measurement problems, each solved independently and in parallel by a single nearest-point query against the surface.

Aside — what breaks the independence, and why you might want it broken

Separability is a property of this particular setting, not a general fact. Impose a limit on what the model may receive — a cap on the weight any region can absorb, or a prescribed distribution over the surface — and the product structure fails. Choosing πᵢ then constrains the choices left for πⱼ, the N problems fuse into a single coupled problem over the joint distribution, and what remains is an optimal transport problem: a global assignment rather than N local queries.

The cost is that a cheap parallel step becomes an expensive coupled solve. What is bought is robustness — a constraint on the receiving side is what prevents many measurements from collapsing onto the same convenient patch of the model, which is the standard failure mode when part of the surface was never measured.

Carry forwardWith the model side unconstrained, the correspondence step is N independent nearest-point queries.

07 — TightnessThe optimal measures are point masses after all

An enlarged search space is only useful if it does not introduce solutions that mean nothing in the original problem. Here it does not, and the reason is a standard fact about linear objectives.

For a fixed pose, the correspondence problem minimizes a linear functional over a convex compact set, and such a minimum is always attained at an extreme point — a point of the set that is not the average of two others. The extreme points of P(Y) are exactly the point masses δ_y: any measure that spreads weight over two or more places is the average of measures that concentrate it differently, and so is not extreme.

The optimum therefore has the original form. All weight sits at one surface location, which is to say the correspondence is a hard assignment. Enlarging the search space admitted nothing spurious.

The one exception is worth naming, because it reappears immediately. A spread-out solution can only be optimal when the minimum of f is attained at more than one location — when there is a tie. Since f(y) = ‖T(x) − y‖², a tie means the transformed measurement has two or more equally near points on the surface. The set of positions in space with that property is the medial axis of Y: the locus running down the middle of a groove, along the ridge above a convex edge, and down the centre of a hole. Off the medial axis the nearest point is unique and the optimal measure is a single point mass. On it, any blend of the tied point masses is equally optimal.

Carry forwardThe relaxation is tight: hard assignment wins, and fractional correspondences appear only for measurements sitting on the medial axis.

08 — EliminationRemoving the correspondences to get a landscape in the pose alone

Two blocks of variables remain: the pose and the correspondences. We eliminate the correspondences and keep the pose, for two reasons. They are cheap to eliminate, since §06 and §07 reduce that step to one nearest-point query per measurement. And they are uninteresting to report, since a list of N surface locations does not answer the question the inspection asked, which is where the part is.

Define the eliminated energy by minimizing (3) over the correspondences at each pose:

E(T)  =  minπ E(T, π₁, …, π_N)
(5)

For any fixed choice of correspondences, the function T ↦ E(T, π) is smooth — a quadratic in the pose parameters, with no combinatorial content left in it. Call each such function a member. Then E is the pointwise minimum of the whole family of members, which is to say their lower envelope.

Envelopes of smooth functions are generally not smooth. Where two members cross, the envelope has a kink — the minimizing correspondence changes as the pose moves through that point, one measurement handing over from one footpoint to another. By §07 those handovers are exactly the poses at which some measurement lands on the medial axis. The discrete structure was not removed by the relaxation; it was converted into the non-smoothness of a landscape defined on six variables, which is a far more tractable form.

09 — TraversalDescending an envelope by minimizing one member at a time

The envelope is non-smooth and is not available in closed form, but it has a structure that makes it easy to descend. Start at a pose T₀ and solve the correspondence problem there, obtaining π*. The member E(·, π*) then has two properties. It lies above the envelope everywhere, because the envelope is the minimum over all correspondences. And it touches the envelope at T₀, because π* is the minimizing choice there.

A smooth function that sits above the objective and touches it at the current point is a majorizer, and minimizing a majorizer cannot make the objective worse. Writing T₁ for the minimizer of the member:

E(T₁)  ≤  E(T₁, π*)  ≤  E(T₀, π*)  =  E(T₀) envelope below member · T₁ minimizes the member · member touches at T₀
(6)

Each iteration decreases the energy, with no step size and no line search. This is the majorize–minimize pattern, and it is what makes the method stable.

The frozen correspondences also give the right derivative rather than an approximation of it. Danskin's theorem states that where the minimizing correspondence is unique, the envelope is differentiable and ∇E(T₀) = ∇_T E(T₀, π*): differentiating the member with the correspondences held fixed produces the exact gradient of the envelope. Where the minimizer is not unique — the handover poses again — the envelope is not differentiable, and the members' gradients span its set of subgradients. That is the kink, seen analytically.

Carry forwardFreezing correspondences and refitting the pose is exact descent on the envelope, not a heuristic alternation.

10 — The algorithmWhat the derivation produces

Assembling §06 through §09 gives the loop.

T ← initial pose

repeat until converged:
    π ← argminπ E(T, π)   # nearest point on Y for each measurement
    T ← argminT E(T, π)   # refit the pose to fixed correspondences

This is the iterative closest point method. The two steps have quite different characters.

The correspondence step is N independent nearest-point queries against the B-rep, by §06, each returning a single surface location, by §07. It is embarrassingly parallel and dominates the runtime, since each query touches the model's faces rather than a fixed-size system.

The pose step minimizes Σᵢ ‖T(xᵢ) − yᵢ‖² over the six pose parameters with the footpoints held fixed. This is the orthogonal Procrustes problem, solved in closed form: centre both point sets, form the 3×3 cross-covariance matrix, and read the rotation off its singular value decomposition. If the rotation is instead linearized about the current pose — or if the residual is measured along the surface normal rather than to the footpoint, which converges considerably faster on nearly flat regions — the step becomes a 6×6 linear system. Either way its cost is fixed and small.

11 — ConvergenceWhat the loop finds, and what it does not

The energy decreases at every iteration and the iterates approach a stationary point of the envelope — in whichever basin the initial pose happened to lie.

Section 05 established that a global minimizer of the relaxed problem exists. Nothing in the derivation connects that minimizer to what this loop returns, and the reason is structural rather than a defect of implementation. The majorizer built at T₀ is assembled entirely out of local queries: each measurement reports only its own nearest surface location, and knows nothing about the alternative it would have chosen under a substantially different pose. A surrogate built from purely local information can only justify a local step, and a method that only takes local steps can only converge locally.

That gap is where the remaining design choices live, and they fall into two kinds.

The first kind searches better without changing the landscape: initialization from features, principal axes, or fiducials to choose the basin deliberately; multiple restarts to sample several basins; outlier rejection to keep a few bad correspondences from steering the pose step.

The second kind changes the landscape itself, and it acts on the block we chose to eliminate. Section 07 showed that when the correspondence problem is left unshaped, its optimum is always a point mass, and the envelope is therefore as kinked as the geometry makes it. Shaping that inner problem changes this. Add a penalty on how concentrated πᵢ may be — an entropy term is the usual choice — and the optimal correspondence becomes genuinely spread out, weighting many surface locations at once. Each member then carries information about a whole neighbourhood of the model rather than a single footpoint, the envelope becomes smoother, shallow basins merge into their neighbours, and the descent can cross regions that would otherwise have trapped it. Reducing the penalty toward zero over the course of the run recovers the exact problem and its sharp optimum, which is the standard annealing schedule. Constraining what the model may receive, as in the aside to §06, shapes the landscape in a related way at a higher price.

Stated compactly: initialization and restarts change where the search starts, and shaping the inner problem changes what the search is moving through. Only the second alters the locality that the elimination introduced.
