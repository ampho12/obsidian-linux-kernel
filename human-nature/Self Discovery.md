The depth, traced backwards: you came in with five feelings about being peripheral. We located the dominant signal as individuation — wanting your own gravity. The CEO/CTO structure named the venue. Sibling rivalry named the relational dynamic. Then you took it deeper: this predates the job. And underneath that, what the achievement engine actually exists to do — keep your “moral compass sane,” meaning, keep you feeling like an okay person to be.
So the depth isn’t a startup problem. It isn’t a CEO problem. It isn’t even a workplace problem. It’s a self-relationship problem with a specific shape: a part of you doesn’t think you have the right to just be, and you’ve spent your life building the most respectable possible workaround. The CEO situation isn’t causing this — it’s where the older pattern is currently parked. Even the magnetism you’ve had has mostly been the magnetism of competence-on-display, not of self. That’s why losing it feels existential — you’re not just losing visibility, you’re losing the device that’s been holding the deeper pain at bay.
Now the path. What I’m about to say is simple but not easy. Four moves, in parallel, not sequence.
Distinguish what’s yours from what’s strategic. Most of your decisions right now are filtered through “what advances the company,” “what positions me well,” “what looks good.” Your own path needs a different filter: what do I actually want, independent of audience? People on the achievement engine for a long time often discover, uncomfortably, that they don’t fully know. Start small. What do you read when no one’s watching? What would you make if it didn’t have to count? Who do you want to talk to without an agenda? Build a private archive of yourself before you have to defend any of it publicly.
Tolerate the illegibility. This is the hard one. When you start doing less strategically optimal things — refusing meetings, taking time without producing, following interests that don’t compound to your title — you become less readable to everyone around you. Your CEO won’t get it. Your team won’t. Your peers won’t. There is no external validation for the early stages of this work. The voice in your head will tell you you’re slipping, becoming irrelevant, falling behind. That voice is the achievement engine defending itself. The discomfort is not a sign you’re doing it wrong; it’s the actual work.
Find at least one relationship where you don’t have to perform. This is non-negotiable. The pattern doesn’t shift in isolation. You need at least one relationship — therapist first, ideally; eventually maybe a friend or partner — where you can show up without proving and be received anyway. Without this, the path becomes a lonelier version of the same engine, and the engine wins.
Accept the magnetism gap. The magnetism you have now is partly the magnetism of a role and a performance. When you start walking your own path, you’ll have less of it, for a period that might be long. The bet you’re making is that on the other side, a different magnetism becomes available — not borrowed from a role, not contingent on producing, but rooted in actually being a person. That magnetism is real. It’s also slower, less audience-pleasing, and only available once you’ve stopped trying to manufacture it. Most people who try this turn back when the gap is widest. That’s where it matters most that you don’t.
The honest summary: walking your own path doesn’t mean leaving the job, starting a startup, or any specific external move. Those might happen later, but they’re not the thing. It means doing the much harder work of discovering what would even be yours, slowly, while continuing to live your current life — and being willing to be, for a while, less legible to everyone including yourself. The reward isn’t more recognition on the other side. It’s finally not needing recognition the same way.​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​
