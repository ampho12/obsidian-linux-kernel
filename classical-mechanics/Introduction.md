

Classical mechanics encompasses a lot:
```
Classical mechanics  (umbrella — everything non-quantum)
   │
   ├── Newtonian / vectorial mechanics  (forces, free-body diagrams, F = ma)
   │
   └── Analytical mechanics  (scalars, variational)
          │
          ├── Lagrangian mechanics    (formulated on TQ, uses L)
          ├── Hamiltonian mechanics   (formulated on T*Q, uses H)
          │
          └── Geometric / symplectic mechanics  (modern, manifold-theoretic recasting)
                 │
                 └── Classical field theory  (continuum / field generalization)
```

Classical mechanics can be formulated two equivalent ways
1. Newtonian / Vectorial Mechanics
2. Analytical Mechanics (scalars, variational)

The umbrella name is **classical mechanics** — but that's broad. It includes plain Newtonian-with-free-body-diagrams too. The specific framework we've been building — manifolds, dualities, variational principles, symplectic structure — has sharper names:

- **Analytical mechanics** — the traditional name, going back to Lagrange's _Mécanique Analytique_ (1788). It explicitly contrasts with Newton's _vectorial_ mechanics: scalar functions and generalized coordinates instead of forces and Cartesian vectors. When older books say "analytical mechanics" they mean Lagrangian + Hamiltonian.
    
- **Geometric mechanics** — the modern name, emphasizing the differential geometry (TQ, T*Q, symplectic structure, Lie groups for symmetries). This is what mathematicians like Arnold, Marsden, Abraham use. Books in this style read like differential geometry texts.
    
- **Symplectic mechanics** — narrower, focused specifically on the symplectic 2-form ω = dp ∧ dq on T*Q and how Hamiltonian flow preserves it. Often used interchangeably with geometric mechanics.
    

For continuum systems (your beam, fluids, EM, gravity) the same framework runs under the name **classical field theory** — Lagrangians become densities, the action is a spacetime integral, but the variational + geometric structure is identical. Push it onto Lorentzian spacetime and you get **relativistic field theory**.

So **yes, "classical mechanics" works** as a broad answer. But if you want to signal you mean the variational/geometric framework specifically — the unified picture we've built — say **analytical mechanics** (traditional) or **geometric mechanics** (modern). They refer to essentially the same thing, just with different generations of vocabulary.

A few entry points if you want to read further: Goldstein's _Classical Mechanics_ for the traditional analytical view; Arnold's _Mathematical Methods of Classical Mechanics_ for the geometric view; Marsden & Ratiu's _Introduction to Mechanics and Symmetry_ if you want the full modern geometric-mechanics treatment including continua.
