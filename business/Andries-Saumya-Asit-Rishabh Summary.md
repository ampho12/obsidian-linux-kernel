# Business Strategy

## The Three Interests

Every business strategy balances three competing interests:

1. **Customer interest** — what they want to use
2. **Founder interest** — what they want to build
3. **Investor interest** — what returns they expect

Strategy lives at the intersection. Ignore any one and the business eventually breaks.

## Validate Before You Build

Once you have an idea, validate it as quickly as possible. Before building, use a render, proposal, mockup, or anything cheap to test it with real customers. Re-align, repeat. If they respond well, build a little more, then re-align again.

Do not assume "if you build it, they will come." At minimum, run the idea past industry professionals and take their feedback seriously. Actively try to _disconfirm_ your assumptions — that is more useful than collecting evidence that flatters them.

A few cautions:

- **Surveys lie.** More people say they will buy something than ever actually do.
- **Market signal is the _what_ and the _why_.** Smart engineers love building and can figure out the "how", but they often build because something is a cool toy, not because someone needs it.
- **The Salesman Trap**: Don't sell everything at once in that if the customer doesn't need what you want, don't immediately say we also do this, we also sell that. Selling everything the customer wants makes you lose credibility. Core competency is key. 

## Narrative

There is **one coherent story**, not three. Start with a few words — something you can say in 30 seconds.

- Don't open with the problem, the gaps, or the technology. No one cares about the technology.
- Lead with what the customer does today and what they will be able to do with your product.
- Sometimes a short product description does all the work:
    - _"A drink that energizes you and helps you burn calories"_ → _"Healthy energy drink."_

Underlying problem: human communication is lossy. There is always a gap between what I think I am saying and what the listener understands. A clear, compressed narrative is the main tool for closing that gap.

## Differentiation

Focus on the _feeling_ you are selling. That feeling usually determines your target audience.

1. **Exclusivity** — Ferrari, Pagani. Small but wealthy customer base.
2. **Cost** — cheap and workable. Wins on volume.
3. **Trust** — when Fanuc moved manufacturing to China, they lost the European market. They were selling trust without realizing it.

Pick one. Trying to sell all three at once usually means selling none of them.

## Focus, Focus, Focus

There is a time and a place for everything. Big companies did not start by trying to do everything.

- **Google** started with a search engine, expanded into adjacent industries, and eventually reached self-driving cars.
- **Amazon** started with online books, moved into general e-commerce, then AWS, and now even groceries.

Regardless of what you are building, ask: what does the consumer actually need? They don't care how big your empire is — they care what problem you are solving for them right now.


## Decision Making

A company cannot be run as a democracy. Decision-making is not a privilege; it is a responsibility. There is real weight to agency, and the repercussions are real.

Every decision needs an owner — someone clearly in charge.

## Robotics-Specific Notes

- The "4th Industrial Revolution" pitch (same machine, multiple processes) is failing in practice.
- The low-cost robotics market is already crowded.
- Any task with a low barrier to entry will get commoditized, one way or another. Build where the moat is.
