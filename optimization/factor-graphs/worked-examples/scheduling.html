Sequencing jobs on one machine

A worked example where the boundary factor is hopeless and stays hopeless — but eliminating just two variables turns out to give an answer that does not depend on the rest of the graph at all. Once that is true, the whole problem collapses to a sort.

The graph

One machine, n jobs, run one at a time with no interruptions. Job j occupies the machine for pj and carries a weight wj saying how much its lateness matters. If Cj is the moment job j finishes, the cost is

Σj wj Cj

Choosing the order is the whole problem. Three jobs to work with:

To make it a factor graph, write the schedule as a sequence of decisions. Let jt be the job placed in position t, and let Tt be the clock reading after t jobs have run. Then two factors per position, and both are familiar:

dt( Tt−1, jt, Tt ) = I (   Tt = Tt−1 + pjt   )
ℓt( jt, Tt ) = wjt · Tt

That is exactly the shape of the control chain — a clock carried forward by a constraint factor, a cost charged at each step, a decision variable hanging off every stage. The clock is the state and the job choice is the control.

But there is one more factor, and it is the same troublemaker as in the matching example: every job must be used exactly once, which is a single +∞ factor touching every jt at once. So the graph is a chain with one wide factor glued across the whole of it.

What elimination costs here

Eliminate from the far end, as with the control chain. The boundary after removing the tail is the clock reading plus the set of jobs already used — and given the set, the clock reading is not extra information, since it is just the total of those jobs' processing times. So the boundary is a subset, and the boundary factor holds one number per subset: 2n of them.

Exact, and no better than the matching problem. Nothing about the elimination order improves it, because the wide factor is attached to everything that remains no matter which end you work from.

Eliminate a window of two

Here is the move. Do not eliminate all the way to the end. Take two adjacent positions, eliminate only those, and keep the entire rest of the graph symbolic — including the clock reading t at which the pair starts.

Suppose the two jobs are i and j. Whichever way round they go, they occupy the machine for pi + pj in total, so the clock afterwards is t + pi + pj either way. The right-hand boundary is identical for both orders, which is what makes the comparison legitimate — the rest of the graph cannot tell the difference.

Only two orders are available inside the window, so the eliminated factor is a minimum over two numbers. Running i first, it finishes at t + pi and j at t + pi + pj:

i then j:    wi(t + pi) + wj(t + pi + pj)
j then i:    wj(t + pj) + wi(t + pj + pi)

Subtract. Every term carrying t appears in both, and so does wipi and wjpj. What is left is two terms:

(i then j) − (j then i) = wj pi − wi pj

The start time is gone. So is everything else in the graph. Which of the two orders is better depends on the two jobs and on nothing else whatsoever.

That is the entire content of this example. An elimination whose answer is independent of its boundary is a decision you can make once, locally, without ever discovering what the boundary is.

On the instance above, with jobs 1 and 2: w2p1 − w1p2 = 2·3 − 1·1 = 5, so putting job 2 first saves 5 — whether the pair starts at t = 0, at t = 2, or anywhere else.

Sort

Rearrange the comparison. Job i belongs before job j exactly when wjpi ≤ wipj, and dividing through by pipj puts it in a form that involves each job separately:

wi / pi  ≥  wj / pj

Each job now carries a single number, and the pairwise rule is just the ordering of those numbers. That consistency is what makes the local decisions compose: take any schedule that is not sorted by w/p, find an adjacent pair in the wrong order, and swap it. The cost does not increase, and nothing outside the pair moves. Repeat until no such pair exists and you have the sorted schedule, which is therefore at least as good as whatever you started with.

Run the jobs in decreasing order of wj / pj.

Smith's rule. Heavy jobs first, long jobs last, and the ratio says how to trade the two off against each other.

On the instance the ratios are 2.00, 0.50, 0.33 for jobs 2, 3, 1, so the order is 2, 3, 1 — finishing at 1, 3, 6 for a cost of 2·1 + 1·3 + 1·6 = 11. All six orders, for comparison:

What this was

The exponential boundary never went away. We simply never had to look at it: a two-variable elimination answered without consulting it, and that was enough to order the whole sequence. After that observation the remaining work is a sort — n log n, no optimization reasoning of any kind.

It is worth being precise about what did the work, because it is fragile. Two things had to hold. The window's two orders had to leave the right-hand boundary identical, so the comparison was allowed to ignore the rest of the graph. And the resulting criterion had to be a consistent ordering of jobs, so that the pairwise decisions could all be satisfied at once.

Break either and the trick evaporates. Give the jobs release times before which they cannot start, or deadlines, and the two orders no longer end at the same clock reading — the comparison starts depending on t, no ordering of jobs can express it, and you are back to a boundary indexed by subsets. Sequencing problems are mostly hard; this one is famous because it is not.
