Matching jobs to workers

A worked example where variable elimination is of no help at all — and pricing the constraint is what rescues it. The graph here is the opposite of a chain: a single factor touches every variable at once.

The graph · What elimination costs here · Price the workers · Reading the prices · What this was

The graph

There are n jobs and m workers. Putting job i on worker k costs c(i, k). Every job needs a worker, and no worker can take two jobs. Minimize the total.

One variable per job, ai, holding the worker it went to. The costs are then unary factors — one per job, each looking at that job alone:

fi(ai) = c(i, ai)

If that were the whole problem the graph would fall into n disconnected pieces and every job would independently take its cheapest worker. Try it on this instance:

c(i, k) worker 1 worker 2 worker 3
job 1428
job 2437
job 3951

Row by row the cheapest choices are 2 + 3 + 1 = 6 — but jobs 1 and 2 both grabbed worker 2, which is not allowed. The rule that forbids it is a constraint, so by the usual device it becomes a factor that is 0 on assignments where all the ai differ and +∞ otherwise.

And there is the problem. That factor does not touch two variables, or three. It touches all of them:

Every scrap of coupling in this problem lives in that one box. Remove it and the problem is trivial; keep it and nothing is local.

What elimination costs here

Run the recipe anyway. To eliminate a3, collect the factors attached to it: f3 and the distinctness factor. The surviving boundary is every other variable in the problem, so the new factor is as wide as the one we started with. The graph did not get smaller. No elimination order fixes this, because there is no order in which that factor is not attached to everything left.

What the remaining problem actually needs to know is less than the full history, though. Assign jobs one at a time and the only thing the rest depends on is which workers are still free. So the honest boundary is a set, and the boundary factor is indexed by subsets:

V(S) = cheapest way to finish the remaining jobs using only the free workers S
V(S) = mink ∈ S [   c(i, k) + V(S \ {k})   ]

That is exact, and it beats trying all m! assignments — but it stores one number per subset, so it is 2m of them. This is the warning from the control chain in its sharpest form: the elimination is correct and the boundary factor is unwritable.

Price the workers

So stop eliminating and use the other tool. The rule being enforced is that worker k is used exactly once, so give each worker a price λk and charge it to whoever takes that worker. The Lagrangian collects into one term per job plus a constant:

L(a, λ) = Σi [   c(i, ai) + λai   ] − Σk λk

Here λk is not a metaphor — it is a price on a worker, in the same units as the costs. And look at what it did to the picture. The one wide factor is gone. In its place is a new variable and one small factor per job:

Now eliminate the jobs. Hold λ symbolic and the ai are no longer tied to each other at all — each one is a separate little problem, and each job simply takes whichever worker is cheapest once the price is added:

q(λ) = Σi mink [   c(i, k) + λk   ] − Σk λk

That is the whole trade. One factor of arity n became one shared variable, and in exchange the elimination that was impossible became n independent minimizations over m numbers each. As always this is the dual order, supλ infa, so q(λ) is a lower bound on the true optimum for every choice of prices.

Reading the prices

Start with all prices at zero. Then q(0) is just the sum of row minima — 6 — the disconnected answer from earlier, now revealed as the weakest possible bound.

Now raise the price of the contested worker. Take λ = (0, 1, 0) and add it to each row:

c(i, k) + λk worker 1 worker 2 worker 3 picks
job 1438worker 2
job 2447worker 1
job 3961worker 3

Each job chose independently, and between them they used each worker exactly once. The choices are feasible by accident of the prices, not because anyone enforced it. Adding up:

q(0,1,0) = (3 + 4 + 1) − (0 + 1 + 0) = 7

And that settles the problem. When the independently priced choices happen to form a valid assignment, the prices paid cancel against the −Σλk, so q(λ) equals the actual cost of that assignment. A lower bound that is achieved by a feasible point is the answer:

7 = q(λ) ≤ d* ≤ p* ≤ cost of that assignment = 7

So the optimum is 7 — job 1 to worker 2, job 2 to worker 1, job 3 to worker 3 — and the three numbers (0, 1, 0) are a proof of it that anyone can check in a few seconds, without searching anything.

Job 2 was indifferent between workers 1 and 2 at those prices, and we broke the tie in the convenient direction. Ties are not a nuisance here, they are the mechanism — the algorithms that find such prices, the Hungarian method and the auction algorithm, spend their time nudging prices until enough ties appear for a feasible set of choices to exist.

What this was

The method did not solve this problem, and that is the point of including it. What it did was locate the difficulty exactly: one factor of full arity, so no elimination order helps and the honest boundary is a subset of workers. That diagnosis is what tells you to stop eliminating and start pricing.

Pricing then did something structural rather than numerical — it disconnected the graph. After that the remaining work is searching for good prices, which is algorithm design, and no more factor-graph reasoning enters.

Worth comparing with the linear-quadratic case. There the boundary factor stayed writable because it never left the quadratic family. Here it stays writable because pricing removed the factor that made it wide. Two different escapes from the same wall.
